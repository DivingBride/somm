# Table 4 - Cooking Transformatio

| Food Ingredient | Raw / Uncooked State | Key Raw Molecules | Cooking Method / Process | Key Transformed Molecules | Aroma Shift | Wine Pairing Implication |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Ginger | Spicy-pungent, citrus, woody | Gingerol / Citral / Zingiberene | Heating / Cooking | Zingerone (sweeter) / Linalool (more prominent) | Spicy becomes sweet-warm; pungency drops significantly | Dry Gewurztraminer works for both raw and cooked; cooked ginger can shift to slightly off-dry Gewurztraminer or Scheurebe; linalool bridge remains constant |
| Ginger | Fresh, spicy | Gingerol | Drying | Shogaol (2x spicier than gingerol) / Gingerol decreases | Spicier, more concentrated | Dried ginger is more intense; wines need more body and aromatic density; still Gewurztraminer/Scheurebe but can handle more alcohol |
| Pineapple | Tropical, fruity (esters dominant) | Ethyl Butanoate / Ethyl Hexanoate / Pentanone | Caramelization | Sotolon-family molecules develop / Furaneol increases | Tropical becomes caramel and curry-adjacent; sotolon family emerges | Raw: Chardonnay. Caramelized: shifts toward Sauternes, amontillado, oloroso -- the sotolon/curry bridge activates |
| Pineapple | Tropical | Ethyl Butanoate | Combined with cloves or vanilla during cooking | Eugenol (from cloves) + Furaneol (from caramelized pineapple) co-dominant | Tropical + cloves + caramel | Now calls for VDN dessert wines (Banyuls, Maury, Rasteau) or Pedro Ximenez rather than Chardonnay |
| Beef | Bloody, metallic, umami | Fatty acids / Amino acids (lower levels) | Boiling / Braising | Amino acids increase (more umami) / Bouillon compounds develop | Blood note disappears; brothy, umami-forward | Opens door to fragrant whites or light-medium reds; wine needs aroma not raw power; even dry Riesling + rosemary stew is viable |
| Beef | Bloody, rich | Fatty acids / Myoglobin (not aromatic) | Grilling / Roasting (high heat, Maillard reaction) | Guaiacol / Pyrazines / Furanic aldehydes / Smoky compounds | Smoke, toast, spice, roasted onion; dramatic aroma increase | Requires full-bodied oak-aged reds (Cabernet Sauvignon, Syrah, Malbec, Tempranillo, Nebbiolo); browning products must match wine's aromatic profile |
| Beef | Raw | Fatty acids (stable) | Dry-aging (28+ days) | Amino acids increase significantly / Fatty acid development | Umami intensifies; deeper, nuttier profile | Demands more powerful wines with hot tannins from Mediterranean climates: Australian/California/Chilean Cabernet, Petite Sirah, Zinfandel |
| Onions / Garlic / Leeks / Shallots | Pungent, sulfurous (raw allicin) | Allicin / Propanethial S-oxide | Caramelizing / Roasting | Syringol (from sugar caramelization) / Maltol / Cyclotene | Sweet, smoky, spicy, caramel | Caramelized alliums develop syringol (wood-lignin family) and maltol; become excellent bridge ingredients to oak-aged wines |
| Oak barrels (new, before wine contact) | Raw wood, tannic | Lignins / Tannins (raw) | Toasting / Charring inside of barrel | Vanillin / Guaiacol / Eugenol / Methyloctalactones / Furfural / Cyclotene / Maltol / Syringol | Oak, vanilla, smoke, coconut, caramel, maple, cloves | Light toast = more fresh wood and lactones; heavy toast = more vanillin, guaiacol, furfural, caramel; American oak requires longer toasting to curb its aromatic impact |
| Maple water (sap) | Very slightly sweet, barely aromatic | Simple sugars / Water | Prolonged heating / Reduction (boiling off water) | Sotolon / Cyclotene / Maltol / Maple Furanones / Benzaldehyde / Eugenol (from maple wood lignins) | Aromatic explosion: maple, caramel, fenugreek, vanilla, caramelized sugar | After transformation, maple syrup pairs with Sauternes, Tokaji, oloroso, vin jaune -- all sotolon-family wines; same aromatic profile as oak-aged wine -- Chartier |
| Fenugreek seeds | Mildly aromatic raw | Proto-sotolon precursors | Roasting | Sotolon (major release) | Becomes the aromatic key to maple syrup; dominant fenugreek = dominant sotolon | Roasted fenugreek in a dish immediately signals: pair with vin jaune, Sauternes, Tokaji, amontillado; the most direct molecular trigger in the book |
| Ceylon cinnamon | Milder, complex | Linalool / Borneol / Eugenol (moderate) / Cinnamic aldehyde (moderate) | Used raw (ground or stick) | Cinnamic aldehyde (dominant) / Eugenol / Linalool all present | Warm, floral, cloves-adjacent cinnamon | Linalool bridge to Gewurztraminer; cinnamic aldehyde to late-harvest whites; eugenol to oaked reds |
| Chinese cinnamon (cassia) | Stronger, spicier | Cinnamic aldehyde (very high) / Less eugenol and linalool than Ceylon | Used raw or in cooking | Cinnamic aldehyde (extremely dominant) | Bold, spicy cinnamon -- less floral nuance than Ceylon | Stronger signal toward Grenache-based reds; late-harvest whites viable but needs robust wines; Rhone or Spanish reds handle the punch |
| Grapes into wine (carbonic maceration) | Grape sugars, phenolics | Grape esters / Anthocyanins | Carbonic maceration (sealed CO2 vessel, whole grapes) | Ethyl cinnamate develops | Fruity, balsamic, cinnamon-touch, amber note | Wines from carbonic maceration (Beaujolais Nouveau) develop ethyl cinnamate -- this is why they pair with cinnamon dishes |
| Any protein (meat, fish, eggs) -- browning | Raw proteins, mild | Amino acids / Sugars | Maillard reaction (dry heat, 140C+) | Dimethylpyrazines / Guaiacol / Furanic aldehydes / Melanoidins | Toasted, coffee, cocoa, caramel, smoke, grilled aromas | Any Maillard-browned food calls for barrel-aged wines (guaiacol, vanillin, furfural bridge) or wines with some toasted-oak character |
| Capsaicin in chili peppers | Burning heat on palate (not an aroma) | Capsaicin / Dihydrocapsaicin | Eating -- no aroma transformation occurs | No aromatic transformation; heat sensation is chemical and physiological only; capsaicin is fat-soluble and spreads in oil-based cooking | Heat increases with oil cooking; dissolves into cooking fat | CRITICAL SERVICE NOTE: Temperature of wine is the key variable -- serve reds at 57-59F (14-15C) with capsaicin; same wine at 18C will seem unbearably hot |
