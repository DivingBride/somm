## Lamb

### Lamb's inherent thymol — the full passage (pp. 92–93)
> "An interesting fact: thymol—the volatile compound responsible for the most important aromatic characteristic of thyme—is also the principal sapid (flavor) molecule contained in lamb. This explains the age-old use of thyme in lamb recipes. On the other hand, this shakes up the idea of the classic pairing between lamb and red Bordeaux wines, in particular those from Pauillac. So, to keep the pairing from being merely by rote—as is the case with Pauillac—instead serve red wines from the Mediterranean basin, marked by aromas of the garrigue (scrublands where herbs such as thyme are abundant). You can do so even if the lamb recipe contains no thyme. After all, the recipe may not contain thyme, but the lamb does!"

*This is the cornerstone passage on lamb. The section heading is "LAMB: A RED MEAT RICH IN... THYME!" Thymol is the dominant sapid molecule in lamb AND the defining compound of thyme. This is why Bordeaux is the wrong call for lamb (it doesn't carry garrigue/thymol character) and why Mediterranean basin reds — Languedoc, Provence, southern Rhône — are the correct pairing. The garrigue scrubland those vines grow in is thyme-dominant landscape, and those aromatic molecules carry into the wine.*

### Boiled/stewed rosemary lamb → Alsatian Riesling (p. 127)
> "Be daring and try boiling lamb in a stew, spiced up with several sprigs of rosemary, and surprise your guests by pairing this red meat with a noble dry Alsatian white Riesling wine. First, boiled meat loses its blood and becomes stringier and very fragrant from the bouillon's flavors, opening the door to pairing with a dry, very fragrant white wine (or with a fruity, biting, very light red wine). Second, the presence of rosemary's major active compounds creates an almost perfect link with those compounds found in dry Alsatian Rieslings. In many cases, German Rieslings have an insufficient alcohol content, and so lack body and density, or may be a bit sweet."

*Context: This is Chartier's signature "surprising" pairing. The word "boiling" is essential — this pairing applies only when the fat character of the lamb has been stripped by boiling. It does not apply to roasted or grilled lamb.*

## Anise / Pastis / Fennel

### Pastis and anethole are richer than fennel (p. 51)
> "Pastis is a liqueur traditionally made from licorice root and star anise. The fruits of star anise are much richer in anethole, a compound with an anise-like taste, than is fennel."

### Braised lamb shank with pastis → Syrah (p. 51)
> "Braise a lamb shank with tomatoes, pastis, and fennel and serve it with a Syrah from the Rhône Valley or from South Africa. Both wines express the same anise tonality found in this lamb dish dominated by pastis and fennel, both of which are rich in anethole."

### Black licorice softens tannins and amplifies red wines (p. 52)
> "Black licorice extract—more precisely, its principal aromatic molecule, glycyrrhizic acid—is added to pastis during the production process in order to smooth it, to give it more presence, and to extend its anethole flavor. Without this acid, pastis would be tasteless and harsh, as are many of the cheap, poor-quality versions. This confirms black licorice extract's power of softening tannins and extending the flavor persistence of tannic red wines."

### Anise-flavored dishes and red wines with anise notes (p. 51)
> "Ingredients in the anise taste family are in perfect harmony with red wines with anise-like notes."

## Rosemary

### Rosemary's terpenes link to Alsatian wines (p. 124)
> "That explains the symbiotic union between rosemary and these wines—not only in theory but also confirmed in practice by our senses."

*Context: "These wines" refers to Riesling and Gewurztraminer, whose terpenic compounds (flower and conifer notes in Riesling; camphor/eucalyptus/cloves/rose in Gewurztraminer) mirror rosemary's volatile terpene family.*

### Rosemary varieties differ in dominant compound (p. 124)
> "There are aromatic differences among different rosemary varieties: Corsican rosemary is richer in borneol (which has a camphor, woody, tonic, and almost medicinal fragrance), with soft, fruity fragrances and a touch of incense. Continental rosemary, from Provence, the southern Rhône Valley, and Languedoc-Roussillon, is characterized by verbenone."

### Rosemary vapors technique and wine service (p. 126)
> "To create an even stronger aromatic link between the fragrances of rosemary and those of your selected wine, serve this dish in a bowl placed in a larger deep dish containing several whole sprigs of fresh rosemary. When it's time to serve your guests, pour a bit of boiling water into the deep dish."

## Mint and Sauvignon Blanc

### Mint and Sauvignon Blanc correspondence (p. 44)
> "We can note, for instance, a particular correspondence between mint and Sauvignon Blanc, as well as among dishes dominated by foods rich in volatile anise-like compounds."

### All meal components should inform the wine selection (p. 47)
> "All too often, wine is chosen to go with the main element of a dish (meat or fish, for example) without consideration of the vegetables, sauce, and other meal components. A fuller understanding of food flavors and wines makes it easier than ever to take into account all components of a given dish. Doing so will increase the meal's harmony and the success of the wine pairing."

## Cold-Tasting Foods

### Cold-tasting compound effects on wine perception (p. 193)
> "A dish composed entirely of cold-tasting ingredients will bring forward the wine's acidity and bitterness (a dry Riesling, nervy and minerally, will become biting and explosive)."

> "If a wine is served too cold when paired with a dish, it will bring forward the food's acidic, salty, and bitter flavors, while calming the syrupy sweetness of sugars."

## Sotolon (Curry / Walnut / Vin Jaune / Sauternes)

### Sotolon discovery and wine connection (p. 58)
> "Over the course of many foggy hours spent decoding wine's volatile compounds, I became aware that the major aromatic note (curry and walnuts) of vin jaune (yellow wine) from Jura, France, originated in 4,5-dimethyl-3-hydroxy-2(5H)-furanone, better known as sotolon."

### Pineapple caramelized → sotolon → Sauternes (p. 108)
> "During cooking, pineapple develops, especially when caramelized, volatile molecules from the sotolon family. Other members of this family include curry, roasted fenugreek seeds, soy sauce, and sweet wines made from grapes subjected to noble rot (botrytis cinerea), such as Sauternes."

### Dishes to pair with sotolon-rich wines (p. 62)
> "Curry-dominated recipes: Pair with a vin jaune or a mature Sauternes."

> "Dishes sweetened with maple syrup: Pair with a mature Tokaji Aszú, a Vino Santo (sweet Italian), a Nigori sake, an oloroso sherry, or a Montilla-Moriles Pedro Ximénez solera."

## Cloves / Eugenol / Oak Barrels

### Cloves and oaked wines share eugenol (p. 112–113)
> "Cloves contain from 70% to 90% eugenol (the principal fragrance of cloves)."

> "Because cloves also contain traces of aromatic compounds such as furfural, caryophyllene, and vanillin... wines raised in [oak barrels] also show strong aromatic traces of cloves."

### Spanish reds and cloves (p. 113)
> "Cloves are Spanish reds based on Garnacha (Grenache) grapes from the Priorat, Rioja Baja, Campo de Borja, and Cariñena regions; wines based on Tempranillo grapes from the Rioja, Toro, and Ribera del Duero regions."

## Riesling and Iodine / Seafood

### Alpha-pinene sublimates iodine (p. 136)
> "Alpha-pinene has the capacity to sublimate iodine's flavor, making it more powerful. Likewise, in wine pairings, the iodine taste of such seafood will intensify if the wine served has powerful pinene aromas. This explains the beautiful union between Riesling and oysters or scampi."

## Pineapple and Strawberries

### Molecular twins despite visual differences (p. 106)
> "Research in molecular biology has shown that pineapple and strawberries, despite their major differences in color and structure, share several volatile compounds that express themselves through numerous identical aromatic molecules."

## Grass-Fed Meat and Terpenes

### Pasture-raised animals and terpene production (p. 89)
> "Grass-fed meat contains a greater quantity of polyunsaturated fatty acids and chlorophyll—which the animals transform into terpenes, a family of aromatic molecules that is strongly present in wine, providing spice and herb notes, among others."

*Context: This is the molecular mechanism behind why pasture-raised lamb, beef, and other animals develop terpene profiles — specifically thymol/carvacrol in lamb — that echo the herbs of their grazing environment and the wines of those same landscapes.*

## Gewurztraminer / Lychee / Ginger

### Lychee and Gewurztraminer as molecular twins (p. 96)
> "When we analyze the aromatic profile of the volatile compounds in lychee and ginger, and of those in white wines made from Gewürztraminer and Scheurebe grapes, we quickly realize their marked similarities."

### Pairing example: Asian chicken with lychees (p. 98)
> "For a true understanding of the potent attraction between lychee and Gewürztraminer, try pairing an entree of Asian-style chicken with hot peppers and lychees and a dry, forceful Alsatian wine such as Gewürztraminer Wintzenheim 2005, Domaine Zind Humbrecht, Alsace."
